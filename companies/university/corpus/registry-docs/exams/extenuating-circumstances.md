# Extenuating circumstances

**Owner:** Examinations & Assessment  
**Last edited by:** Ethan Marchetti (Assessment Officer)

---

**What counts as an extenuating circumstance?**

Something unexpected, outside your control, and that genuinely affected your ability to prepare for or sit an assessment. Examples include a serious illness or injury, hospital admission, bereavement of a close family member, or a significant personal crisis. Not included: long-term conditions already covered by a disability support plan, minor illness that did not stop you sitting the exam, poor time management, or events you knew about well in advance (for example, a holiday booked before the exam timetable was published).

**How do I submit a claim?**

Through the Student Hub. Go to the extenuating circumstances section, select the assessments affected, describe what happened, and upload your evidence. The system will confirm receipt and assign your case a reference number. You can also submit on paper at the Student Services reception on the Hollins Hill campus, but the online route is faster and lets you track progress.

**When is the deadline?**

Claims should be submitted within five working days of the assessment or of the event affecting you, whichever is later. If you cannot meet that deadline, submit as soon as you can and explain the delay in the form. The panel can consider late claims where there is a good reason.

**What evidence do I need?**

Written evidence from someone independent who can confirm what happened. For illness, a medical certificate or a letter from your GP or the university's Student Health Service. For bereavement, a copy of the death notice or a letter from a faith leader or funeral director. For other circumstances, a letter from a professional who was involved (a counsellor, a social worker, a manager if you work). Evidence should be dated and name you.

**What if I only had a short illness?**

For illness of up to seven days, you can self-certify. The Student Hub will generate a self-certification form you sign and upload. You do not need a medical certificate. This covers things like a bad cold, flu, or a stomach bug that stopped you sitting an exam. You can self-certify at most twice in an academic year; further claims need third-party evidence.

**Who looks at my claim?**

Each faculty runs its own panel. Panels sit twice in each assessment period, so there is usually a decision within about a week of the panel date. The panel includes the faculty's assistant dean, a member of Examinations & Assessment, and an academic from a different school in the faculty. You will be told the outcome through the Student Hub.

**What are the possible outcomes?**

The most common are:

- **Extension** – for coursework or other non-timed assessments. The panel sets a new deadline, normally 7 to 14 days after the original one.
- **Deferral** – for exams or timed assessments. Your assessment is moved to the next available sitting, usually the supplementary period. The mark is uncapped, as this is your first attempt.
- **No action** – if the panel decides the circumstances did not significantly affect you.
- **Withdrawal from the assessment** – in rare cases where you were unable to sit and it would be unfair to require a deferral.

**Will a deferral show on my transcript?**

No. A deferral because of extenuating circumstances is recorded internally but does not appear on your transcript or degree certificate. It is not the same as a fail or a resit.

**Can I appeal the panel's decision?**

Yes. If you think the panel made a procedural error or ignored evidence, you can ask the Academic Registrar to review the decision. Send your request through the Student Hub within ten working days of the outcome. The review considers whether the process was followed correctly, not whether the panel should have reached a different conclusion.

**Where can I get advice before submitting?**

The Students' Union advice service can help you prepare your claim and evidence. Student Services can also talk you through the process. If you are not sure whether something counts, it is better to submit and let the panel decide than to hold back.

**What about exams I have already sat?**

Claims about exams you sat are only considered where something happened during the exam itself (for example, you were taken ill at your desk) or where you had good reason not to submit beforehand. The panel will look at the evidence and may decide a deferral is appropriate even though you completed the paper.

**How does this interact with the exam board?**

The panel's decision is sent to the exam board. If your deferral is approved, your result for that assessment is recorded as deferred and the board does not classify it. If you were unable to submit a dissertation or major project, the board may recommend you take the deferred assessment early so it can be considered at the next main board rather than the supplementary one.
