# Annual reviews and goals

All Kellsbrook Health staff take part in the annual review cycle, which runs on the July financial year. The cycle is owned by Human Resources, and managers own the actual conversations with their teams. Every employee has a review period that starts with the first full pay period of the new financial year and runs for twelve months, with a formal review write-up due at the end of that period.

## The review cycle

The cycle has two formal touchpoints. The first is a mid-year check-in, due around the end of the calendar year, where the manager and employee review progress against the goals set at the start of the period, adjust those goals if the work has shifted, and discuss anything that needs more support. The check-in is a conversation, not a written review — managers are expected to log it in the HR system but there is no formal rating attached.

The second touchpoint is the annual review itself, which managers write up during the first month of the new financial year. The written review covers three areas: how well the employee met their goals, how they performed against the competencies for their role, and any notable contributions or concerns that came up during the year. The manager shares the draft with the employee, they meet to discuss it, and the employee adds their own comments before the review is finalised.

## Goals

Goals are set at the start of each review period by the manager and employee together. Good goals are specific to the employee's role, measurable where that is possible, and aligned to the priorities of their team and the wider organisation. A clinic nurse's goals will look different from a claims examiner's or a research coordinator's, and that is expected.

Goals should be recorded in the HR system within the first month of the review period. If a goal changes mid-year — the work moved, the project was deprioritised, the employee changed roles — the manager updates the goal in the system and notes the reason. It is better to update a goal than to leave a stale one in place and review against work nobody is doing.

## Competencies

Competencies describe how the work gets done, not just what gets done. For clinical roles (nursing, allied health, medical staff) the competency set includes patient safety, documentation practice, communication with patients and families, and collaboration with the care team. These map closely to the behaviours covered in the clinical ladder and to the standards in the nursing handoff guidance.

For non-clinical roles (finance, IT, research administration, member services, and so on) the competencies are: reliability and follow-through, quality of work, communication, collaboration, and customer focus — where the customer may be a patient, a member, a provider, or another Kellsbrook team. Some departments add a technical competency specific to their function; the HR business partner for that area can help shape these.

Employees in research roles also have a set of research-specific expectations around good clinical practice, data integrity and study documentation. These are covered in the research institute's training programme and are reflected in the review competencies for those roles.

## Merit increases

Merit increases are set once a year, effective at the start of the new financial year. The size of the increase is driven by two things: the employee's overall rating from their annual review, and where their pay sits in the range for their role. A rating of "exceeds expectations" puts the employee at the top of the merit range for their role; "meets expectations" puts them in the middle; "needs improvement" puts them at the bottom or, in some cases, means no increase at all.

The rating definitions are in the HR system and a manager can look them up at any time, not just at review season. Budgets for merit are set by the executive team each spring, and HR publishes the range for each rating before managers start writing their reviews.

## Raising concerns

If an employee disagrees with their review, or feels the process was not followed fairly, they should first raise it with their manager. If that does not resolve it, they can speak to their HR business partner or to anyone in Human Resources. Reviews can be corrected if a fact is wrong; opinions and ratings are harder to change but the concern is still heard and considered.

Employees who believe a manager is not taking the review process seriously — skipping check-ins, writing reviews without a conversation, or rating against criteria that do not match the role — should raise that with HR too. The review cycle only works if it is real, and Human Resources treats concerns about it the same way it treats any other workplace concern.

For questions about the review cycle, goals, competencies or merit, contact Human Resources at hr@kellsbrookhealth.org or find your HR business partner in the HR system directory.
